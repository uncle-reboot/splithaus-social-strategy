What changed in v5 (team feedback, Sep 28)

  • Two houses at a time, not five. Launch with Loon Point (family) and the Quarterdeck (friends). A new region gets its own series only after the first two prove out, one at a time, not interleaved.
  • A mix of family and friends. Loon Point carries the heartstrings; the Quarterdeck carries present-day comedy.
  • The Quarterdeck is now set in 2026. The 1979 friends just sold it to Ray's son Danny and three friend households who are figuring out, right now, how to share it. No heartstring arcs. It is also the most natural home for SplitHaus.
  • Fair Winds is retired. A family shrinking to five isn't a SplitHaus story.
  • Agate House and Windhill are on the bench, canon kept, for after the 90-day review.
  • The next 10 episodes use only the two launch houses (v4 introduced all five within the first 10).
  • Video: route shots by type and check continuity. From the model test: the problems people noticed were continuity errors (the loon walking onto land, June's cane vanishing), not image quality.

Executive summary

No ads. We build a following, and our own characters, with storybook houses that narrate the families and friends who share them. Playful on the surface, heartstrings underneath.

What

Two houses to start. Loon Point: one big family, 70 summers, told with time travel. The Quarterdeck: four friend households in their first year together, told in the present.

Why these two

Family and friends are both how people share a place. The pair gives us heart (Loon Point) and comedy (the Quarterdeck), and the Quarterdeck is exactly the situation SplitHaus is built for.

Why characters

Cheaper, no uncanny-valley backlash, Instagram is cracking down on fake AI people, and characters are IP we own.

How it grows

Made to be sent to the family chat or the friend chat, which is how Instagram decides who else sees it. New regions are added one at a time, only after these two work.

Cadence

About 5 feed posts a week (2 episodes, 2 static, 1 community) plus daily Stories.

Cost

About $70–90/month in generation and tools. About 2 hours/week of review and 20 minutes/day of community replies.

Story rules (the canon bible)

Tone: storybook, not reality TV

  • Loon Point carries the heart. The Quarterdeck is present-day comedy: light nods to the past, no heartstring arcs.
  • No illness arcs, lawsuits, divorces, inheritance fights or real-world tragedies.
  • Every character is loved by the narrator, including the difficult ones.
  • The Quarterdeck shows friends working out time, money and rules; never legal, tax or financing advice about co-owning.

The house narrates

  • The house is always the narrator, in first person: 'All the things I've seen.'
  • It only remembers what happened in or around it.
  • One voice per house, locked before launch.
  • Characters speak in short lines; the house does most of the telling.

Time travel

  • Loon Point uses it in most episodes; the Quarterdeck only as a quick comic comparison ('In 1979…').
  • Every memory is dated on screen. Each era has a look: 1950s–60s Kodachrome, 1970s faded warm, 1990s camcorder, today in full color.
  • Memories must match the canon timeline; past characters (young June and Walt, the 1979 friends) are cast once and reused.

Staying consistent

  • A canon log: every episode records its years, facts, new rules and character changes.
  • The universe runs in real time; ages advance every summer.
  • Character reference sheets are locked; new outfits are logged.
  • Style: felt stop-motion by default; a softer 3D storybook look is allowed if it tests better. Never photoreal people.

The houses

Two launch houses, deliberately different: a family and a friend group, the past and the present, heart and comedy.

The universal conflict: One house. Everyone who loves it. Never enough of it to go around. Every story comes from the same year-round tension: who gets time at the house, who does the work, who makes the rules, and who carries it forward. Each house plays it differently, so there's always a new story: holidays, weekends, repairs, newcomers, traditions, babies, goodbyes.

The Cabin at Loon Point · since 1956

Shape: The classic family sitcom. Matriarch plus two married siblings' households, cousins, and a first fiancé arriving. Most comedy.

Central conflict: Whose turn is it?. Two married siblings, their spouses, their kids and a matriarch who wants everyone there at once. Time at the cabin is the currency, all year long.

Storylines it creates, year-round: Who gets the Fourth of July · Opening weekend and closing weekend · Thanksgiving at a closed-up cabin · A wedding on the dock (on whose week?) · Who actually does the work (Ruth) · The kids are adults now and want equal turns · Bringing a fiancé into the rotation

Time-travel device: The kitchen door frame. Pencil height marks since 1956: every child, every dog, one very tall boyfriend in 1991.

Narrator voice: Warm, dry, older man. Unhurried, faintly amused. Has seen 70 summers of family politics and loves every one.

Season: Summer peak; opening day in May, closing day in October.

Canon timeline

  • 1956 Walt Halloran and June build the cabin and the dock with their own hands. On the Fourth of July, Walt finishes the dock and proposes on it. They marry that fall.
  • 1966 First height mark on the door frame: 'Dave, 1966'.
  • 1978 The famous 1978 photo: every Halloran on the dock at once. It hasn't happened since.
  • 1994 Walt's last Fourth of July. Dave has 'had the Fourth' ever since.
  • 2006 Maya's first swim; Theo falls off the dock (twice).
  • 2026 Kayla brings her fiancé Marcus up for the first time. The Fourth dispute goes to arbitration.
July 1979: the original eight friends write the napkin. The house brings it up whenever the new crew argues.
Lake Chelan, Eastern Washington

The Quarterdeck · since 1979

Shape: The present-day friends story. For 47 years, four friends ran this house on a 1979 bar napkin. This fall they sold it to Ray's son Danny and three friend households who are figuring out, right now, how to share one house without losing the friendship. Comedy, not heartstrings.

Central conflict: Friends, or co-owners? Can four friend households share one house and still be friends?

Storylines it creates, year-round: Whose weekend is it (and who reads the group chat) · Who pays for the dock, the propane, the boat · Party weekends vs. toddler weekends · The originals dropping by with advice · Kyle's spreadsheet vs. the napkin · Picking weeks for their first summer · Who's invited to the sunset toast

Time-travel device: Two napkins. The 1979 original hangs framed by the door; the new crew's napkin hangs next to it, and every argument ends with a new line. The house compares them ('In 1979 they settled this with a pitcher'). Used for laughs, not nostalgia.

Narrator voice: Easygoing, sun-baked, a little 70s. Spent 47 years with one friend group and is now breaking in a new one. Calls everyone 'kid'.

Season: Year-round, in the present: summer weekends, harvest and wine weekends in fall, snowy winter planning.

Canon timeline

  • 1979 Four young couples write a contract on a bar napkin at the Blue Heron Tavern and buy the lake house that fall. Article 1: 'Everybody gets a quarter.'
  • 1981 Article 7: 'Whoever breaks the boat fixes the boat.' (Skip.)
  • 1996 The originals' kids take over the bunk room. Danny Castillo, 18, grills his first burger on the deck.
  • 2024 The Jorgensens retire to Arizona. For the rest of the originals, the stairs to the dock start 'getting longer'.
  • Spring 2026 The originals decide it's time to sell. Danny can't stand to see it go to strangers and talks three friend households into buying it together.
  • Sep 2026 Closing day. The new crew writes their own napkin. Article 1: 'The originals get the first week of July. Forever.'
  • Now Their first winter as co-owners. Kyle has a 14-tab spreadsheet. Nobody else has opened it.

Platforms show more of what people send, comment on, re-watch and come back for. Every format below causes one of those four. No "comment YES if…" bait, which Meta demotes; every question has a real answer we want.

How SplitHaus shows up

Casually, the way people mention the app they actually use. Part of the house's life, not the point of the story.

Rules

  • Loon Point: about 1 episode in 5. The Quarterdeck: up to 1 in 3, because sharing logistics are its story. Never in a heartstring episode.
  • One line maximum, usually the setup for a joke.
  • Only the organizers use it (Ruth and Kyle), which is true to who buys it.
  • Only show what SplitHaus actually does (schedules and fair turns). Never imply features it doesn't have, like splitting bills.
  • No calls to action inside the story; the bio link and newsletter do that. The house can be skeptical of it; that's funnier.

Example lines

  • Loon Point: 'This year Ruth put the rules somewhere everyone could see them. She calls it SplitHaus. I call it peace and quiet.'
