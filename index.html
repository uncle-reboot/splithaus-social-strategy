The Houses

Five family places, each narrating everything it has seen: the summers, the fights, the rules, the people who are gone and the ones just arriving.

Because the houses remember, we can go back to any year they've stood.

What changed in v3

  • Five houses, not three. Added Agate House (Oregon coast, 1921, six generations; the long-running flagship saga) and The Quarterdeck (Lake Chelan, Eastern Washington, 1979; a multi-generational friend group, not a family).
  • Every house has a canon backstory and timeline so episodes stay consistent over years of posting.
  • Time travel is a core device. Each house narrates from memory and can go back to any year; each era has its own look (sepia, Kodachrome, VHS).
  • Multi-family casts. Grandparents, married siblings and their spouses, cousins, a fiancé, step-family, and friends' kids and grandkids.
  • More difference between houses: 12 characters vs. 5; a saga vs. a sitcom vs. a friend group; different family shapes, conflicts, tones and seasons.
  • Style is flexible. Felt stop-motion is the default look, not a rule. Never photoreal humans.
  • SplitHaus is woven in casually, about 1 episode in 5, never as an ad.
  • An interactivity playbook built to drive comments, sends and repeat views.

Executive summary

No ads. We build a following, and our own IP, with a storybook universe of family places narrated by the houses themselves. Playful on the surface, heartstrings underneath: more time at the place you love.

What

Five houses in five regions, each with its own family (or friends), its own voice, its own central fight and a hundred years of memories to draw on.

Why it works

Our audience is older, nostalgic, family-centered. The content is made to be sent to the family group chat, which is how Instagram decides who else sees it.

Why characters

Cheaper, no uncanny-valley backlash, Instagram is cracking down on fake AI people, and characters are IP we own.

Why time travel

A house that remembers can tell a 1921 story and a 2026 story in the same 30 seconds. Endless episodes, and the nostalgia is built into the format.

Cadence

About 5 feed posts a week (2 episodes, 2 static, 1 community) plus daily Stories. Two houses active at a time.

Cost

About $50–100/month in generation and tools. About 2 hours/week of review and 20 minutes/day of community replies.

Story rules (the canon bible)

Tone: storybook, not reality TV

  • Heart without heaviness. People we've lost are remembered warmly, off-screen and long ago.
  • No illness arcs, lawsuits, divorces, inheritance fights or real-world tragedies.
  • Every character is loved by the narrator, including the difficult ones.
  • Comedy comes from specific, true-to-life family behavior; heart comes from time passing.

The house narrates

  • The house is always the narrator, in first person: 'All the things I've seen.'
  • It can only remember what happened inside or around it (its point of view keeps stories grounded).
  • Each house has one voice, locked before launch, never changed.
  • Characters speak in short lines; the house does most of the telling.

Time travel conventions

  • Every memory is dated on screen (a small, handwritten year).
  • Each era has a look: 1920s sepia · 1950s–60s Kodachrome · 1970s faded warm · 1980s–90s camcorder · 2000s early digital · today, full color.
  • Memories must match the canon timeline. Past characters are cast once (young June, Walt, Nora…) and reused.
  • The present always answers the past: the memory explains something happening today.

Staying consistent

  • A canon ledger: every episode logs its years, facts, new rules and character changes.
  • Ages advance one year every real summer; the universe runs in real time.
  • Character reference sheets are locked; new outfits are logged.
  • Style: felt stop-motion by default; a softer 3D storybook look is allowed if it tests better. Never photoreal people.

Oregon coast · fictional town of Tillicum Bay

Agate House · since 1921

Shape: The saga. Biggest cast, two joined families (Whitlock + Reyes), SIX generations across 105 years. Our long-running flagship.

The central fight: Who rings the bell. The brass ship's bell is rung when the last person arrives, so everyone fights to not be last, and to be the one who rings it.

Time-travel device: A jar for every year. Nora started a jar of agates in 1921; every summer since, the family fills one jar. 105 jars on the windowsills. Any jar can open a memory (built-in time travel).

Narrator voice: Old, gentle, a little formal, salted by a century of weather. Speaks slowly, like someone who has watched a lot of tides. Calls everyone by their full first name.

Season: Year-round (the Oregon coast has no off-season, just fog). Peaks: summer, storm-watching winters, Thanksgiving.

Canon timeline

  • 1921 Samuel Whitlock, a retired ship's mate, and Nora build the house. Nora hangs the bell from Samuel's last ship and starts the first agate jar.
  • 1934 Arthur Whitlock, age 11, rings the bell for the first time and stays the family's official bell-ringer for the next 74 years.
  • 1946 Hal Whitlock born; spends every summer of his childhood at the house.
  • 1962 The Big Blow (Columbus Day storm): the whole family sleeps in the parlor by lantern light. The house considers it its finest night.
  • 1971 Hal marries Rosie on the porch. It rains. Nobody minds.
  • 1998 Daniel and Keiko, Sarah and Luis: two young families, one kitchen. The 'two-week swap' system is invented.
  • 2008 Arthur's last summer. He rings the bell one final time.
  • 2026 Baby Nora born, named for her great-great-great-great-grandmother: the sixth generation. Jar #106 begins.

Northwoods lake country (Minnesota / Wisconsin)

The Cabin at Loon Point · since 1956

Shape: The classic family sitcom. Matriarch plus two married siblings' households, cousins, and a first fiancé arriving. Most comedy.

The central fight: Who gets the Fourth of July.

Time-travel device: The kitchen door frame. Pencil height marks since 1956: every child, every dog, one very tall boyfriend in 1991.

Narrator voice: Warm, dry, older man. Unhurried, faintly amused. Has seen 70 summers of family politics and loves every one.

Season: Summer peak; opening day in May, closing day in October.

Canon timeline

  • 1956 Walt and June Halloran buy the lot and build the cabin and dock with their own hands.
  • 1966 First height mark on the door frame: 'Dave, 1966'.
  • 1978 The famous 1978 photo: every Halloran on the dock at once. It hasn't happened since.
  • 1994 Walt's last Fourth of July. Dave has 'had the Fourth' ever since.
  • 2006 Maya's first swim; Theo falls off the dock (twice).
  • 2026 Kayla brings her fiancé Marcus up for the first time. The Fourth dispute goes to arbitration.
Vermont ski country

Windhill · since 1971

Shape: The blended family. A founder, his sister, his son's second marriage, a stepdaughter with a toddler, a competitive nephew. Rivalry and warmth.

The central fight: Who gets Christmas week, and who skis vs. who's on toddler duty on a powder day.

Time-travel device: The yardstick on the deck. Big Tom nailed it up in 1971; every big storm is measured and carved into it with the date.

Narrator voice: Crisp, cheerful, a little vain. An A-frame who still thinks it's the coolest house on the mountain. Speaks in snow reports.

Season: Winter peak: first snow, Christmas, Presidents' Day. Quiet, green summers (a secret favorite).

Canon timeline

  • 1971 Big Tom Lindqvist and his two brothers build the A-frame in one summer. His sister Bev supervises from a lawn chair.
  • 1978 The Blizzard of '78: 41 inches on the yardstick. The house's favorite number.
  • 1991 Tom's son Erik (then 20) meets his first wife on the chairlift. Ellie is born the next year.
  • 2014 Erik marries Priya. She reorganizes the mudroom on day one; Tom sulks for a year.
  • 2024 Ellie's son Bo is born. First mitten stolen by Moose within the week.
  • 2026 Tom turns 74 and still skis first chair, mostly.

Cape Cod, Massachusetts

Fair Winds · since 1932

Shape: The small one. Five people and a seagull. A 92-year-old founder, his daughter, her two very different daughters, and a great-uncle who never left. Intimate, tender, funny.

The central fight: Who gets August (and whether Uncle Richie is ever actually leaving).

Time-travel device: Pop's Log. Frank has written one line in a logbook every evening of every summer since 1958.

Narrator voice: Salty, gentle, romantic. A 1932 cottage who measures time in tides and sand on the floor.

Season: Summer peak, with a February–April planning season when the family fights over August.

Canon timeline

  • 1932 Fair Winds is built by a sea captain's widow.
  • 1958 Frank 'Pop' Costa and Dot buy it as newlyweds with every cent they have. Frank starts his log.
  • 1958 Dot's baby brother Richie, age 1, spends his first summer. He has never missed one.
  • 1963 Carol born; her first summer is a week later.
  • 1999 Hurricane season scare: the family boards the windows together and holds a candle-lit card game.
  • 2019 Dot's last summer. Her rule, 'the house belongs to whoever shows up', is laminated by Carol.
  • 2026 Pop is 92. Meg booked August in January. Annie says she'll 'just come when it's nice'.
July 1979 — eight friends, one tavern table, one napkin.
Lake Chelan, Eastern Washington

The Quarterdeck · since 1979

Shape: Not a family: a friend group. Four couples bought it together in 1979 on a napkin contract. Now it's the original friends in their 70s, their grown kids and their grandkids, all 'family' by choice.

The central fight: The napkin rules. Four quarters, four families, one lake. Article 4: 'Nobody sells without all four.'

Time-travel device: The Napkin. The 1979 bar-napkin contract hangs framed by the door. Every rule the friends ever made is a new line squeezed onto it.

Narrator voice: Easygoing, sun-baked, a little 70s. Calls everyone 'kid'. Thinks the napkin is the greatest legal document ever written.

Season: Summer peak (hot, dry, blue water); harvest and wine weekends in fall; Opening Day in May.

Canon timeline

  • 1979 Four young couples (the Novaks, Ostranders, Castillos and Jorgensens) split a pitcher at the Blue Heron Tavern and write the napkin contract. They buy the lake house that fall.
  • 1981 Article 7 added: 'Whoever breaks the boat fixes the boat.' (Skip.)
  • 1996 The second generation takes over the kids' bunk room and the ski boat; Article 12: 'No teenagers drive the boat after dark.'
  • 2019 Gus Ostrander passes; Marlene keeps his quarter and his chair.
  • 2021 Connie Castillo passes. Ray still sets a place for her at the first dinner of summer.
  • 2024 The Jorgensens retire to Arizona; son Kyle inherits the Jorgensen quarter and a lot of opinions.
  • 2026 Kyle proposes 'modernizing' the napkin. Skip says over his dead body. Marlene says that can be arranged.

Interactivity: how we drive views

Platforms show more of what people send, comment on, re-watch and come back for. Every format below is designed to cause one of those four. We avoid "comment YES if…" bait, which Meta actively demotes; every question we ask has a real answer we want.

Ask the House (comment a year)

Followers comment any year the house has stood; the house answers with a reply-video that shows the commenter's name. Agate House alone has 105 years to pick from. The strongest repeatable engine: every answer invites more comments.
Mechanic: Comments + reply videos (IG and TikTok both support replying to a comment with a Reel/video).

You decide the ending

Cliffhanger episodes (Ep. 6) end on a question; a 48-hour Stories poll decides; we post the winning ending. Viewers come back to see if they won.
Mechanic: Stories polls + follow-up Reel.

Your rule becomes canon

Followers submit real house rules and traditions; the best become canon, woven into an episode or a character's laminated sheet, with credit.
Mechanic: Form in the bio + DMs; credit on screen.

Hidden things, on purpose

Walter the loon, Gerald, Moose's mitten and the sun doodle are hidden in every episode. 'Spot Gerald' comments drive re-watches, which platforms reward.
Mechanic: Recurring Easter eggs.

The cross-house mystery

A small sun doodle appears in every house's guestbook across decades. Who drew it? Fans theorize for months. (Canon answer: Rosie Whitlock. She signed Loon Point's guestbook from her 1960s bookmobile, Fair Winds' and Windhill's on road trips, and doodled on the Quarterdeck napkin in 1979. Revealed around episode 40.)
Mechanic: Lore posts + 'theories' comments.

Tag / send prompts

Every episode ends on a specific send: 'Send this to your Dave', 'Tag who would sign your napkin', 'Send this to your Pop'. Sends are the #1 signal Instagram uses to reach new people.
Mechanic: Caption + final line.

Which character are you?

Quizzes and 'which house are you' polls across five houses and 40+ characters. Cheap, weekly, very shareable.
Mechanic: Stories quiz stickers + carousels.

Name it

Occasional naming votes: the next Windhill puppy, Skip's new (used) boat, the Loon Point 2027 baby. Low effort, high participation.
Mechanic: Stories polls.

Guestbook

Followers send a photo of their family place; the house 'remembers' it, redrawn in our style (with permission).
Mechanic: DM submissions + weekly feature.

Broadcast channel: Letters from the Houses

An Instagram broadcast channel and newsletter for superfans: early episodes, votes, behind-the-scenes, canon notes. Builds an owned audience.
Mechanic: IG broadcast channel + email.
Priority order: (1) end every episode on a send/tag line; (2) launch Ask the House with Ep. 4; (3) run You decide the ending with Ep. 6; (4) plant hidden things from Ep. 1; (5) start the sun-doodle mystery at Ep. 9. Measure each by comments per 1,000 views and sends per reach.

How SplitHaus shows up

Casually, the way a real family mentions the app they use. It's part of the house's life, not the point of the story.

Rules

  • About 1 episode in 5. Never in a heartstring episode.
  • One line maximum. It's usually the setup for a joke.
  • Only the organizer characters use it (Ruth, Priya, Carol, Kyle), which is true to who buys it.
  • No calls to action inside the story; the bio link and the newsletter do that job.
  • The house can be skeptical of it; that's funnier and more believable.

Example lines

  • Loon Point: 'This year Ruth put the rules somewhere everyone could see them. She calls it SplitHaus. I call it peace and quiet.'
  • Windhill: Tom: 'What's that?' Priya: 'SplitHaus.' Tom: 'Hmph.' (Later he checks it when nobody's looking.)
  • Quarterdeck: Kyle: 'I'm not replacing the napkin. I'm backing it up.' House: 'They kept both.'
  • Fair Winds: Carol laminates a printout of the August schedule. Richie: 'You know that's on your phone.' Carol: 'Now it's on the fridge.'
